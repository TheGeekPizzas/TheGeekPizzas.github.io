The History of Pizza

From Ancient Flatbreads to a Global Culinary Icon

Ancient Origins

The story of pizza begins long before tomatoes were ever introduced to Europe. Ancient civilizations across the Mediterranean, including the Egyptians, Greeks, and Romans, regularly baked flatbreads topped with olive oil, herbs, garlic, and cheese. In ancient Greece, citizens ate a flat bread called plakous, which was flavored with toppings like onions, herbs, and garlic.

Roman soldiers and peasants consumed similar flatbreads known as panis focacius, the direct ancestor of modern Italian focaccia. These early dishes served both as a meal and as an edible plate for other foods.

The Birth of Modern Pizza in Naples

Modern pizza as we recognize it today originated in Naples, Italy, during the 18th century. Naples was a bustling, densely populated port city where working-class residents, known as lazzaroni, needed inexpensive food that could be eaten quickly while walking or working outdoors.

Street vendors sold flatbreads topped with simple ingredients such as garlic, lard, salt, caciocavallo cheese, and basil. When tomatoes—brought to Europe from the Americas in the 16th century—were finally embraced by Neapolitans as a safe and tasty ingredient in the late 1700s, the classic tomato-topped pizza was born.

The Royal Legend of Pizza Margherita

In 1889, King Umberto I and Queen Margherita of Savoy visited Naples. According to popular culinary legend, the Queen grew tired of the heavy French cuisine served at court and requested an assortment of pizzas from Pizzeria Brandi, run by baker Raffaele Esposito.

Esposito prepared three varieties, but the Queen favored one topped with red tomatoes, white mozzarella cheese, and green basil—ingredients chosen to represent the colors of the newly unified Italian flag. In her honor, this creation was named Pizza Margherita, cementing its place in history.

Crossing the Atlantic to America

During the late 19th and early 20th centuries, millions of Italian immigrants arrived in the United States, bringing their culinary traditions with them. In 1905, Gennaro Lombardi opened the first licensed pizzeria in America, Lombardi's, in New York City.

Initially, pizza remained popular primarily within Italian-American neighborhoods. However, after World War II, American soldiers returning from Italy brought back a strong appetite for the dish. Pizzerias quickly spread across the nation, giving rise to distinct regional styles such as New York-style thin crust, Chicago deep-dish, and Detroit-style pizza.

A Worldwide Phenomenon

By the second half of the 20th century, refrigeration, frozen pizza, and international delivery chains transformed pizza into one of the most recognized foods on Earth. In 2017, UNESCO officially recognized the traditional art of the Neapolitan pizza maker (Pizzaiuolo) as part of the world's Intangible Cultural Heritage.
